01

Primary finding

TARS needs lift as well as rotation

The most important lesson is that the walking mechanism needs a hidden translational degree of freedom near the leg joints. Rotation alone does not create enough clearance for the legs and torso to pass one another.

Design consequence: GP-TARS V2 must reserve a vertical lift/translation path through the central joint region. This is now treated as a core gait requirement rather than an optional refinement.
02

Failure mode

Why rotation-only walking jams

Diaz's early full-scale mechanism reproduced the rotary motion but omitted the lift mechanism. The legs contacted the ground and the structure could not continue the walking cycle. Enlarging the axle channels so the leg assembly could move vertically relative to the centre torso created the clearance needed for forward stepping.

03

Mass and impact

Heavy torso = violent steps

Diaz reported that excess battery mass and poor weight distribution produced hard steps and repeated drivetrain breakage. GP-TARS therefore keeps the battery as low and central as possible, avoids unnecessary upper-body mass, and treats landing loads as a structural design case rather than only a motor-sizing problem.

Keep mass lowBattery and dense power hardware stay close to the lower centreline.
Avoid torso-heavy packagingCompute and display are kept as light as practical.
Design for landingBrackets, shafts and bearings must tolerate repeated impact loads.
Control the descentThe gait should actively manage contact rather than simply drop onto the next support face.
04

Gait sequencing

Walking is a coordinated state machine

The Hackster build's movement sequence reinforces our ROS 2 state-machine approach. The torso height and rotation are coordinated so that TARS creates clearance, pivots, establishes contact and returns to a stable pose.

  1. 01Raise / shift torso
  2. 02Create rotational clearance
  3. 03Rotate torso / leg section
  4. 04Control pivot
  5. 05Establish ground contact
  6. 06Transfer load
  7. 07Return height
  8. 08Recover neutral
  9. 09Verify stability
05

Surface contact

Landing geometry matters

Diaz refined his gait so the torso contacted the ground more flush rather than landing and sliding. GP-TARS should likewise use controlled contact geometry, sole/body shape and feedback to reduce uncontrolled sliding across surfaces with different friction.

07

Design changes adopted

Changes to GP-TARS V2

  • Add a vertical lift/translation degree of freedom to the central walking mechanism
  • Reserve CAD clearance for leg sections to pass the centre torso during rotation
  • Model lift, rotation and recovery as separate coordinated gait states
  • Keep the battery and other dense components low and central
  • Include landing and recovery impacts in structural and actuator load calculations
  • Use controlled ground contact instead of uncontrolled drops or slides
  • Validate gait first on a restrained test fixture before free walking
Source: Charlie Diaz, “How to build your own replica of TARS from Interstellar,” Hackster.io. The GP-TARS design adapts the lessons rather than copying the original miniature servo architecture.
